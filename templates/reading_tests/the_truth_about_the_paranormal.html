Telepathy

Reading Passage 1 — Questions 1–13

A Principles of telepathy and parapsychology have split the scientific community for decades. Skeptics point out that after more than a century of intensive research, there is still no clear evolutionary or physical explanation of how one human brain could communicate directly with another. However, recent rigorous laboratory trials have provided a growing volume of evidence suggesting that telepathy might actually exist.

B Throughout the history of parapsychology, early practitioners were criticized for utilizing loose methodology. Many experiments relied heavily on manual card-guessing tasks where the researcher could inadvertently signal the correct answer to the test subject. The breakthrough came when researchers developed the ganzfeld experiments—a highly controlled approach named after the German term for 'entire field'. In these tests, targets are selected completely at random by computers to ensure total academic integrity, and subjects are placed in a state of sensory deprivation to minimize external environmental interference.

C The basic setup of a modern ganzfeld experiment is carefully standardized. The person acting as the 'receiver' is placed in an isolated room, sitting in a comfortable reclining chair. They wear headphones playing uniform white noise and have their eyes covered with halved ping-pong balls under a soothing red light. In a completely separate, soundproofed room, another person acts as the 'sender' and attempts to mentally transmit a randomly chosen visual target—such as a specific picture, image, or short video clip—directly to the receiver's mind.

D Once the transmission session concludes, the receiver is presented with four distinct visual options. One of these options is the true target, while the remaining three act as completely unrelated dummy selections. The receiver is then asked to identify which image most closely matches the psychological impressions they experienced during the deprivation session. Statistically, if telepathy does not exist, a subject should naturally select the correct target by pure chance exactly 25 per cent of the time.

E Over several hundred individual trials, researchers discovered that the actual success rate across various laboratories reached over 32 per cent. This margin represents a statistically significant deviation that cannot be easily dismissed as mere luck. However, some traditional mainstream scientists remained intensely skeptical, suggesting that subtle flaws in experimental design—such as the sender's monitor leaking a faint signal, or researchers accidentally giving out clues—might account for the higher success rates.

F To completely rule out these potential leaks, parapsychologists developed an automated variation known as the 'autoganzfeld' experiment. In this format, the selection, presentation, and evaluation of visual targets are fully controlled by sophisticated computer programs. Humans are removed from the technical workflow entirely, preventing any accidental sensory leakage. Despite these rigorous automated protections, the overall hit rate remained consistently above the 25 per cent baseline, proving that automated setups do not eliminate the phenomenon.
