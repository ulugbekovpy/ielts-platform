The Mystery of Migratory Birds

A Every year, billions of birds embark on an incredible journey across the globe. From the tiny Arctic Tern flying from the Arctic to the Antarctic and back, to large raptors navigating thousands of miles, avian migration remains one of nature’s most fascinating spectacles. For decades, scientists have puzzled over how these creatures manage such precise navigation without the aid of modern instruments.

B Recent studies suggest that birds possess a biological "compass" that allows them to detect the Earth’s magnetic field. This ability, known as magnetoreception, is believed to be linked to specialized proteins in the bird’s eye, which allow them to "see" magnetic lines. Furthermore, they use a combination of celestial cues, such as the position of the sun and stars, and environmental landmarks to adjust their course during flight.

C Migration is not solely an instinctual drive; it is also a learned behavior. Juvenile birds often follow their parents on their first migration, absorbing knowledge about essential stopover points—wetlands, forests, and islands where they can rest and refuel. The loss of these habitats due to urban development and climate change has led to a alarming decline in many migratory species.

D As the climate shifts, migratory patterns are changing. Some species are arriving at their breeding grounds earlier than before, which can create a mismatch between their arrival and the peak availability of food. Protecting migratory corridors has become a global priority, as these long-distance travelers are critical for ecosystem health, acting as pollinators and seed dispersers.
